Roomie · UX case study

Fair isn’t equal.

A household app for roommates in Kathmandu that makes fairness visible. It weighs chores by effort, bends around busy weeks, and sends the reminder itself, so nobody has to be the one who asks.

Problem

One person quietly does more

Roommates stay fair with memory, WhatsApp and a chart on the fridge. When schedules change, one person absorbs the extra work, and asking for help sounds like an accusation.

Every shared flat already has a system. It just runs on memory.

Roomie is a mobile app for students, young professionals and friends sharing a flat in Kathmandu, Lalitpur and Bhaktapur. Today their household runs on a WhatsApp group, a verbal agreement, and whoever notices the overflowing bin first.

The mission, from the brand deck: “create roommate harmony without the passive-aggressive sticky notes.”

Freemium, sold per household

Free forever: chores, pantry list, reminders. Roomie Plus later adds smarter automation. Every new household brings in two or three more users through invites.

NPR, eSewa & WhatsApp

Payments already happen in eSewa, Khalti and Fonepay. Coordination already happens in WhatsApp. Roomie has to be an upgrade on both habits, not another step.

Solo, 2 weeks, no engineers

Research, strategy and UI were all mine. Every feature had to justify its build cost on paper first, which made prioritisation a real design tool.

The gap

A chores-and-expenses app already exists. What no app does is plan around people’s real weeks and explain why the split is fair. The strongest competitor isn’t an app at all: it’s a free group chat everyone already uses.

Insight

“All-in-one” isn’t a differentiator. Flatastic already combines chores, shopping and expenses.

Decision

Position Roomie as a neutral third party that carries the mental and social load, not as a tracker.

Result

Every later feature had to answer one question: does this remove a remembering or an awkward conversation?

02

Define

Turning scattered findings into one sharp problem: fairness is invisible, and enforcing it is socially expensive.

Affinity map: five themes

I clustered the review findings, competitor gaps and root-cause chains until the same five problems kept showing up.

The silent burden

One person notices everything and ends up doing it.

Awkward money

Asking for Rs. 150 back costs more socially than it’s worth.

Grocery confusion

Shared or personal? Already bought? Nobody knows.

Real life

Exams, late shifts and travel break fixed rotas.

Mental load

Coordination lives in memory and a chat that scrolls away.

Four insights

1

Fairness is effort, not count

A 2-minute trash run and a 45-minute bathroom clean are not the same. Counting tasks hides the imbalance.

2

The real chore is remembering

Planning, assigning and following up is invisible work, and it usually lands on one person.

3

A reminder from a friend reads as criticism

Every root-cause chain ended the same way: there’s no neutral intermediary.
Hypothesis · to validate

4

Automation is trusted only when it explains itself

“Why me, why this week” has to be answerable in one sentence.

  3. Why aren’t they tracked?People rely on memory, chats and manual effort.

As-is chore flow: the memory and anxiety loop

Without a system, one missed chore forces a choice between two bad exits. Rahul either sends an awkward text and creates tension, or does the chore himself while resentment builds. Both roads end in the same place, and the loop starts again next week.

  • No record of who owns what, so it all runs on memory.
  • The person who notices becomes the enforcer.
  • There is no neutral way out: every exit costs the relationship something.

Problem statement

Roommates struggle to manage shared chores and groceries fairly because they rely on memory, scattered chats and manual coordination. As schedules change, responsibility blurs, creating mental load, awkward conversations and repeated friction.
Core HMW · How might we help roommates share a home in a way that feels fair, effortless, and free from awkward conversations?
HMW make chore distribution feel fair despite changing schedules?
HMW reduce the mental load of remembering, coordinating and following up?
HMW let the system do the reminding, so nobody has to?

To-be chore flow: zero nagging

Smart Division removes the “Remind him?” decision entirely. Nobody has to choose between tension and silence, because the app owns the reminder and the fallback.

  • The nudge comes from Roomie, in the tone each person picked.
  • A missed chore doesn’t become a confrontation. It becomes an open task anyone can claim.
  • Extra effort is remembered and balanced in next week’s plan, so the helper isn’t silently out of pocket.

Money is already served

Splitwise and eSewa are good enough. The chore half is where nothing works.

Money needs a high trust bar

Reviews show financial features only earn trust with complete, explainable breakdowns. A half-built ledger would lose more trust than it earns.

Rules before AI

05

Test

Roomie hasn’t been tested with users yet. This is the plan I’d run next, and what each result would change.

Round 1: moderated usability test

Who: 6 people who share a flat in the Kathmandu Valley: 2 who usually end up doing the extra work, 2 with unpredictable schedules, and 2 who tried a chore app or rota and gave up. No friends or bootcamp classmates; they’re too kind to the design.

How: 40 minutes each on the Figma prototype, thinking aloud. It opens with “tell me about the last time a chore got missed at home”. Tasks are situations, not screen names, and I don’t help. I note what people do (pause, backtrack, reach for WhatsApp) before what they say.

Riskiest assumption

Do streaks and “effort debt” motivate, or shame?

The research warns against ranking people, but V1 resets streaks and gives a heavier load to whoever misses tasks. I’d show the streak-reset and effort-debt screens in both nudge styles, watch the first reaction, then ask one neutral question:

Tell me what this screen is saying about you.

The target is zero. One “judged” gets probed; if 2 of 6 describe it as blame or punishment, streaks become opt-in and effort debt becomes private.

What the results would change

A task fails

Fix the screen and retest with 5 new people before moving on.

The pool reads as blame

Back to Define. The “neutral third party” insight is wrong, not just the UI.

Only minor friction

Log it for the backlog and move to Round 2.

Round 2

Concierge pilot. Run the Sunday chore plan by hand for two households over WhatsApp for two weeks.

Why

It tests the core bet (does a neutral planner reduce friction?) before any code exists.

Measure

Reminders sent person to person (target: zero), plans accepted without edits, and how each household describes the fortnight in a closing call.

06

Reflect

What this project taught me as a graphic designer moving into product.

Thinking

Fair isn’t equal changed how I design

My first instinct was to make chores look nice. The real work was defining what “fair” means and making it visible: effort chips, busy days, and a pool with no names attached.

Trade-offs

Cutting my top-scoring feature

RICE said build expenses first. Context said the money half is already solved and needs a trust bar I couldn’t reach in two weeks. The scores informed the decision; they didn’t make it.

Next time

Put it in front of people sooner

My evidence comes from public reviews, competitor products and structured reasoning. It’s real, but it isn’t the people who’ll use Roomie. Next time I’d test the concept before hi-fi. That would also have caught two inconsistencies still visible in these screens: the roommates screen lists “Mumbai” in a Kathmandu product, and the profile screen shows a household “rank”, which cuts against my own no-ranking principle. A third slipped through export: the Home screen’s nav bar highlights Chores.
